# Craft notes: where the method comes from

The method in [METHOD.md](METHOD.md) follows what stand-up and humor-writing sources broadly agree on. This page lists the building blocks, a reading of how well-known comedians use them, and the sources. It describes technique only: no one's material is reproduced, and storycraft doesn't imitate any comedian's voice or lines.

**How reliable is this?** The building blocks follow the sources at the bottom. The comedian-by-comedian reading is the author's own analysis from general knowledge and wasn't checked against a source; treat it as a hypothesis to test by listening.

## Building blocks

| part | what it does |
|---|---|
| **Setup** | Creates an expectation. Short, concrete, doesn't spoil the point. |
| **Pivot** | The hinge: the sentence that could mean two things. |
| **Punchline** | Breaks the expectation. The surprising word comes last. |
| **Tag** | Another laugh on the same setup with almost no new setup. Tags stack, each landing faster. |
| **Callback** | Return to an earlier joke much later; rewards memory, gives closure. |
| **Rule of three** | Two items build a pattern, the third breaks it. |
| **Misdirection** | Make the listener sure where it's going, then be somewhere else. Bad misdirection is just confusing. |

## What the story engine adds

1. A one-sentence premise. 2. Specific, not general, details. 3. The narrator is the fool. 4. Escalation in threes. 5. A turn that recasts what came before. 6. Quiet before the reveal. 7. A button. 8. A true thing underneath. 9. Organize by subject or theme, not chronology; the strongest specials snap several threads into one arc.

## A reading of well-known comedians (author's analysis, unsourced)

| comedian | craft move worth studying |
|---|---|
| Jerry Seinfeld | Obsessive economy; a tiny everyday premise examined until it turns absurd. |
| Richard Pryor | Acts the scene out, character to character, and goes straight at a painful truth so the laugh is a release. |
| George Carlin | Language as the subject: taxonomies, wordplay, a rant that builds logically to a crescendo. |
| Mitch Hedberg | One-liners that bend a phrase sideways, deadpan, with long pauses. |
| John Mulaney | Long stories with precise, escalating detail, strong tags at every step, returns to earlier details. |
| Bill Burr | The reasonable man losing his temper; the rant climbs from a fair point to an absurd one and implicates himself. |
| Dave Chappelle | Slow-burn storytelling: long setup, a narrator who isn't the hero, an ending that reframes. |
| Louis C.K. | Opens with a confession and escalates the honesty. |
| Ali Wong | A set built as a story with several plot threads snapped together. |

## Sources

- Chris Head, how to tell a story in stand-up, Chortle (social storytellers reveal the point too early; misdirection).
- The Pudding, *More story structure* (comedians build multi-plot stories; analysis of Ali Wong's *Baby Cobra*), and the Funny How dissection of the same special.
- University of Bologna, *American stand-up comedy as storytelling* (tension and release).
- General comedy-writing guides on setup, pivot, punchline, tags, callbacks, rule of three and misdirection (quality varies; the clearer ones were used).
