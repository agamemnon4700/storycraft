# The method: point first, built backwards

A joke is a sentence. A story that makes people laugh *and* mean something is an argument in disguise. This is how to build one on purpose.

## 1. Point

Decide the true thing the story is for, in one sentence: *waiting is easier with company; good enough is a decision; soon is a mood, not a time.* Write it down. **Do not put it in the story until the last line**, and then say it in the narrator's own words, not as a moral. If you can state the point without the story, the story isn't doing anything. If a listener can state the point after, without being told, it worked.

## 2. Frame

The false belief the narrator starts with, which the story will prove wrong: *the tile comes Tuesday; two weeks means two weeks; I'm just a guest.* The laugh lives in the gap between the frame and the truth. A good frame is something the narrator is a little too sure of.

## 3. Engine

How the gap widens. Pick one:

| engine | the move |
|---|---|
| **denial** | the narrator keeps believing the frame while the evidence piles up |
| **escalation** | each piece of evidence is bigger or more absurd than the last |
| **literalism** | someone takes a phrase or instruction exactly literally |
| **scale** | a gap in size or importance between the narrator and what he is dealing with |

## 4. Beats

`hook -> specific -> test1 -> test2 -> test3 -> turn -> button`

- **hook**: one sentence; tells the listener what kind of story this is.
- **specific**: one concrete detail ("a guy who sells socks out of a suitcase", not "a guy").
- **test1, test2, test3**: three pieces of evidence the frame is false. Each is funnier than the last, and the third is the most absurd. (Two build the pattern; the third breaks it.)
- **turn**: the narrator half sees the point. A beat of quiet before it.
- **button**: the point, short, said in the narrator's own wrong-footed way, as the last words. End on the punchline word and stop. Don't explain.

Rules of thumb: one to two sentences per beat; plain words, contractions; the narrator is the fool (safe, warm, never cruel); one twist per beat.

## 5. Checks (before it is spoken)

Every beat present; short; nothing cruel; nothing that explains the joke; no invented people or places beyond the fixed cast and the facts you were given; a real fact used when facts were given; the button is a finished sentence. These live in `engine.check`. They keep a story clean and honest and cannot make it funny.

## 6. Delivery

For a voice (or stage directions): heat builds across the three tests and drops for the turn and the button; the pause before the turn is the longest; the pace slows at the turn and button. These are the `DELIVERY` hints.

| beat | heat | pause before | pace | mood |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| hook | 0.10 | 0.00 | 1.00 | |
| specific | 0.10 | 0.10 | 1.00 | |
| test1 | 0.20 | 0.15 | 1.00 | |
| test2 | 0.45 | 0.15 | 1.03 | sarcastic |
| test3 | 0.70 | 0.20 | 1.06 | sarcastic |
| turn | 0.00 | 0.55 | 0.90 | serious |
| button | 0.00 | 0.35 | 0.92 | |

## Serials

A running gag is a callback machine. Keep a short ledger of the beats already told so a series never contradicts itself, move the saga one step per telling, and end each episode on a *different* point. The example serial shows five.

## Worked example

Point: *Waiting is easier with company.* Frame: *the tile comes Tuesday.* Engine: escalation.

> **hook** Update on the tile.
> **specific** It's coming from Italy, which the contractor says like it's a place you can drive to.
> **test1** Tuesday, the tile is in customs.
> **test2** Thursday, the tile is on a boat. A different boat.
> **test3** Friday, the boat has a name now, it's a very nice name, and I don't like where this is going.
> **turn** Then you made yourself a cup of tea and sat down next to me, and I forgot about the tile for an hour.
> **button** Waiting's not so bad with company. Don't tell the tile.
